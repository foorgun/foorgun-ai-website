The most common reason automation projects disappoint isn't the technology. It's that nobody can say exactly **how the workflow actually works.** So a process gets automated that three people do differently – and the result is faster, but not better.

That's why every automation starts with an unglamorous step: **write the workflow down once.** It sounds trivial, but it's often the moment the real problems become visible.

## Why document first

An unclear process doesn't get clearer through automation, only faster. When you write the workflow down, three things happen:

- **You see the gaps.** Who does what when the responsible person is on holiday? What happens with an exception?
- **You find the time sinks.** Often the lost time isn't in the work itself, but in waiting and in re-typing.
- **You can decide what's worth it.** Not every step needs automating. Some simply disappear once the workflow is clearer.

## What "document" means here

Not a 50-page manual. To start, **one page per workflow** is enough. The goal isn't completeness, it's that a second person understands the process without asking.

Five pieces of information will do:

| Question | Example |
|---|---|
| What triggers the workflow? | An enquiry arrives by email |
| Which steps follow, in what order? | Read, transfer data, write quote, follow up |
| Who is involved? | Office, management |
| Where is a decision made? | Approve the price before sending |
| What's the result? | The quote is out and logged in the CRM |

Write it down the way it **actually works today** – not the way the process manual says it should. The gap between the two is often half the insight.

## Spotting the weak points

Once the workflow is on paper, go through it with four questions:

- **Media breaks:** Is data re-typed from one system into the next? Every switch between email, Excel and tool is a source of errors.
- **Waiting time:** Where does something sit because someone is waiting for an approval or a reply?
- **Duplicate work:** Is the same information maintained in several places?
- **Exceptions:** What happens when something doesn't match the standard? This is exactly where automated workflows fail later.

The points where several of these come together are your best candidates for automation.

## When BPMN or EPC makes sense

For most small workflows a numbered list is enough. But as soon as a process has **branches** ("if it's a new client, then …, otherwise …") or **several people** across departments, a simple diagram helps more than text.

There are two common notations for this: BPMN and the EPC. You don't need to master either – the point is that everyone shares the same picture of the workflow. The key symbols are covered in [BPMN explained simply](/blog/bpmn-explained-simply).

## Common mistakes

- **Starting too detailed.** Better the rough workflow on one page than every edge case on ten.
- **Writing down the ideal** instead of reality. What gets automated is what actually happens.
- **Documenting alone.** The person who does the workflow every day knows the exceptions the boss doesn't see.
- **Stopping at documentation.** Writing it down is the start, not the goal.

## And then?

Once the workflow is clear, the decision becomes easy: which step costs the most time, has clear rules and connects cleanly? **That's where you start** – with the smallest thing that noticeably takes work off your plate.
